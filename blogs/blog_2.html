Gender Bias: Why It Is Caused and How We Will Solve It

Thoughts on inclusivity, stereotypes, and empowering women in STEM

The Global Challenge

Beyond the hard truth of underprivileged students in countries that lack proper STEM curriculums, there is another massive problem, even in schools that have achieved a healthy STEM environment. The reality is that women make up only 35% of all STEM graduates worldwide. Gender inequality in STEM remains a staggering global challenge.

Why Does This Happen?

According to UNESCO's latest article on Advancing Gender Equality in STEM Education: Inspiring Girls to Pursue Science, we need to realize that the root cause from which all other problems stem is early stereotypes. Children absorb societal messages by the first grade, wrongly viewing math and physics as inherently masculine fields, or believing that hands on work is only for men. Let's be honest: you wouldn't be as surprised if a friend bought your son a science kit for his birthday as you would be if it was directed at your daughter.

Another critical reason is a sharp drop in confidence. Girls often show lower self efficacy in math and science despite achieving equal or better grades than boys, and in many cases, they are outperforming them.

These two factors lead to a far smaller number of well known, awarded female representatives compared to boys. This lack of visible role models acts as a self fulfilling prophecy, often discouraging young girls from pursuing the STEM path.

How We Will Solve It

However, we are not here just to showcase the problem; we are here to find a solution. Already, educational departments in the EU and USA, alongside various non profit organizations, are starting girl centered hackathons. These events give girls the chance to feel comfortable in a space tailored for them to grow, learn, and experiment. Additionally, mentorship programs that connect students with active female role models have proven to massively boost academic confidence.

But change also starts at a foundational level. It requires early hands on play, giving girls the science toys and building blocks that society usually reserves for boys. It requires inclusive teaching. Teachers must actively use gender neutral methods, eliminate bias in their grading, and make a conscious effort to highlight historical women in science. A highly effective practice is using International Women's Day not just as a holiday, but as a dedicated time to showcase awarded women in STEM and their monumental contributions to society.

So, go on. Start your girls only club or organize a girls' hackathon to empower the STEM girlies in your local community! The tools are out there, and the change starts with us.
